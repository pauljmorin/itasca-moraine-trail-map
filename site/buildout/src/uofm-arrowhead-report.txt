I. Introduction
The North Country National Scenic Trail (NCT) is the longest continuous hiking trail in the
United States, extending more than 4,800 miles across eight states and connecting a remarkable
diversity of ecological, cultural, and recreational landscapes. In Minnesota, roughly 859 miles of
the route traverse the northern half of the state, incorporating established segments such as the
Superior Hiking Trail and the Border Route Trail. Despite this progress, significant gaps remain
where the trail has not yet been constructed as a permanent, natural-surface route. One of the
highest priorities is the segment between Grand Rapids and Snowbank Lake, also referred to as
the Arrowhead region, where approximately 150 miles of the current alignment rely heavily on
road walks and temporary connections rather than purpose-built hiking trails.

In the Arrowhead region, the present state of the trail is characterized by long stretches along
state and county highways and local roads. These segments diminish scenic quality, increase
exposure to vehicle traffic, and fragment the sense of immersion that long-distance hikers expect
from a National Scenic Trail. At present, there is an active, long-running volunteer effort to
identify and secure off-road reroutes. Volunteers, chapter leaders, and partner agencies have
sketched preliminary route ideas, walked candidate corridors, and discussed possibilities with
land managers, but they have had limited access to a unified geospatial framework that allows
them to evaluate options across the entire corridor in a consistent way.

Through the University of Minnesota’s GIS 8501 course, 18 Master of Geographic Information
Science (MGIS) students formed North Star Consulting to support the North Country Trail
Association (NCTA) in advancing this work. Over the course of a semester, the team developed a
geospatial workflow, suitability model, and suite of map products designed to highlight where
the landscape is most favorable for an off-road NCT alignment and where major constraints
remain. The analysis emphasizes features that are desirable for trail development such as public
land, existing non-motorized trails, campgrounds, and bridge crossings, while identifying
features that are difficult or undesirable to cross, including private parcels, wetlands, large water
bodies, major roads, and portions of multi-use trails that emphasize motorized or paved use.

This report summarizes that work, describing the study area and objectives, explaining how the
suitability model and least-cost path analysis were constructed, and presenting results for three
focus areas: Grand Rapids, the central Arrowhead region around the Taconite State Trail, and the
Ely area near Snowbank Lake. The report concludes with a discussion of tools delivered to
NCTA, limitations of the model, and recommendations for future ground-truthing, partnership
development, and higher-resolution re-modeling. This report intended to complement the project
StoryMap and web maps and to serve as a baseline document for future phases of route planning
and environmental review.

II. Project Objectives and Study Area
At the beginning of the course,the class identified a shared set of objectives for the North
Country Trail project. The first objective was to compile a standardized collection of
environmental, infrastructural, and ownership datasets for the Grand Rapids–Snowbank Lake
corridor, drawing on Minnesota Geospatial Commons, MnDOT resources, and data shared by
such as the North Country Trail Association and state agencies. Second, the project aimed to
construct a suitability model that classifies the landscape into more and less appropriate locations
for a natural-surface hiking trail, based on factors such as public versus private ownership,
proximity to existing trails, and the presence of hydrologic barriers. Third, the team aimed to use
that suitability surface to generate candidate trail alignments through least-cost path analysis
between key anchor points. Finally, the project sought to deliver web and desktop tools
(shapefiles, KMLs, web maps, and summary tables) that NCTA and local volunteers can use in
ongoing route evaluation, ground-truthing, and environmental documentation.

The study area is defined by the NCT’s revised preferred alternative corridor between Grand
Rapids and Snowbank Lake. This corridor polygon, provided by NCTA, represents a broad
swath of land north of the existing trail within which a future permanent route is desired (Figure
1). To account for edge effects and provide routing flexibility around the corridor boundary,
North Star Consulting worked within a buffered version of this corridor that extends modestly
beyond the preferred alignment. The primary counties intersecting this area of interest are Itasca,
St. Louis, and Lake Counties, with a small overlap into Aitkin County.

To keep the analysis focused and computationally efficient, parcels and other land-based datasets
were restricted to the counties that clearly intersect the main routing direction between Grand
Rapids and Snowbank Lake. Parcel data from opt-in open data counties and an additional Itasca
parcel dataset were integrated to provide the best available coverage. Because Aitkin County’s
overlap with the corridor is minimal and lies south of the primary east–west routing direction, its
parcels were not included in the final suitability model.

Within this corridor, the analysis focuses particularly on three subregions: Grand Rapids, the
central Arrowhead region around the Taconite State Trail, and the Ely area near Snowbank Lake.
Near Grand Rapids, the trail already uses a mix of off-road segments and road walks as it
approaches and leaves the city. In the central Arrowhead, the current alignment relies heavily on
roads but runs near the Taconite State Trail and other public lands that offer opportunities for
rerouting. Near Ely and Snowbank Lake, the corridor is constrained by a combination of private
land patterns and transportation infrastructure, making partnership with existing right-of-way
(ROW) managers especially important.


III. Methods
In order to identify candidate NCT alignments within the corridor, North Star Consulting
developed a raster-based suitability model and least-cost path workflow. The analytical
framework is based on two main steps. First, diverse datasets representing ownership, recreation
infrastructure, and environmental constraints were translated into a common ordinal suitability
scale. Second, these datasets were combined into a composite cost surface that guides least-cost
path analysis between chosen locations on the trail.

The suitability model uses a seven-class scale, where low values represent highly suitable
locations, values in the middle of the scale represent neutral or moderately suitable areas, and
higher values represent increasingly unsuitable or effectively excluded locations. For example,
existing non-motorized hiking trails, state forest campgrounds, and public lands were generally
assigned lower values, while private parcels, wetlands, lakes, and road surfaces were assigned
higher values.. For each input layer, the team created a consistent classification field and applied
reclassification rules that reflected the expected feasibility and desirability of routing the trail
through those features (Figure 2).

Data acquisition and preparation followed a consistent pattern. First, statewide or regional
datasets were gathered from Minnesota Geospatial Commons, MnDOT, and other open sources.
These included parcel layers from opt-in counties, a statewide trails layer, a state forest
campground dataset, a hydrology dataset containing rivers, lakes, and wetlands, and a MnDOT
bridge inventory, among others (Appendix A). All layers were reprojected to a common
coordinate system (NAD 1983 UTM Zone 15N) to ensure alignment during overlay and raster
conversion. Each dataset was then clipped to the buffered NCT corridor or to the intersecting

                                                                                                   5
counties so that only features relevant to the study area remained. This reduced processing time
and avoided including features far outside the corridor that would not reasonably influence the
final alignment.




Figure 2. Data flow diagram data and methods used to create the suitability map and trail route model.

The parcel dataset required particular pre-processing. Parcels from open-data counties were
combined with Itasca County parcels obtained from another source. Ownership codes were
generalized into a smaller number of categories reflecting their likely feasibility for trail
development. Lands held by federal, state, county, or similar public entities were grouped into a
“public” category that received favorable suitability scores. Regional or special-purpose lands,
such as local authorities or institutional holdings, were grouped into a more neutral category.
Private parcels were placed in a category that received relatively unfavorable scores, reflecting
the negotiation and uncertainty inherent in securing access across them. Aitkin County parcels
were not brought into the final classification, in order to maintain focus on the main alignment
between Grand Rapids and Snowbank Lake rather than encouraging large southerly detours.

Existing hiking trails were processed to highlight non-motorized hiking infrastructure that could
potentially be shared with the NCT. Trails coded as allowing hiking were selected and clipped to
the corridor. To allow for slight deviations around the mapped alignments, the trails were
buffered by a small distance before conversion to raster. Both the trail polylines and their buffers
were translated into cells with low suitability values that encourage the model to use these
corridors wherever possible.

Campgrounds were processed as attractive nodes along the potential route. State forest
campgrounds and day-use sites were reviewed, and sites used exclusively for horses or day use
were removed. Remaining campgrounds were clipped to the corridor, buffered to represent the
broader area within walking distance of each site, and assigned favorable suitability values that
encourage routes to favor those areas. These buffered polygons were converted to raster and
incorporated into the composite cost surface.


                                                                                                         6
Bridges were treated as key crossing opportunities over rivers and highways. The MnDOT
bridge inventory was filtered to retain only pedestrian and mixed-use structures, then clipped to
the corridor, then buffered to create a small zone within which the model could approach the
crossing. Buffers were ranked based on whether the structure primarily served pedestrians or a
mix of highway and pedestrian use, and whether it crossed a water feature. This ranking gave the
highest preference to purely non-motorized water crossings, while still recognizing the value of
shared-use bridges that provide safe, established crossings. The buffers were converted to raster
and added to the cost surface as favorable cells around viable crossing locations.

Hydrologic features were treated largely as constraints. Rivers, lakes, and wetlands were selected
where they intersect the corridor then converted into a raster with high suitability scores that
discourage routing through them, except where bridge buffers provide designated crossings. This
approach reduces the likelihood that a modeled route will propose crossing open water or
saturated ground at locations where no practical bridge or boardwalk is present.

After all layers were prepared and reclassified, they were converted to a common 30-meter raster
resolution and combined into a composite cost surface using raster algebra. This step effectively
stacked the influences of ownership, hydrology, trails, campgrounds, and bridges. Areas where
multiple favorable features overlapped, for example public lands near an existing trail and a
campground, received low combined cost values, while areas with overlapping constraints, such
as private parcels covered by wetlands, received high cost values. The resulting surface
describes, cell by cell, the relative “effort” required to construct and manage a trail.

Least-cost path analysis was then used to derive candidate NCT alignments. For this project,
anchor points were placed where the current NCT changes from road walks to more suitable
natural-surface segments and at key locations where major route decisions must be made along
the corridor. Using the composite cost surface, the least-cost path algorithm identified routes that
minimize cumulative cost while remaining within the corridor. These raster paths were converted
back to vector polylines, visually reviewed, and compared with the current road-based alignment
and with preliminary routes that volunteers had previously drafted.

Although the model provides powerful screening capabilities, it has important limitations. The
choice of 30-meter resolution balances regional coverage with computation time but cannot
capture fine-scale microtopography, small streams, or short steep slopes that may matter to field
crews. The seven-class suitability scale compresses complex factors into a limited set of
categories; for example, not all private land is equally difficult to negotiate, and not all public
land is equally suitable for trail development. The model also relies on the best available open
data at a single point in time, and it does not include confidential environmental or cultural
resource datasets that would need to be considered in formal environmental review. These
limitations reinforce that the modeled route is a tool for screening and prioritization rather than a
final decision.

IV. Results
The modeling results can be understood at two scales. At the corridor scale, the suitability
surface distinguishes broad trends of opportunity from broad trends of constraint, helping
planners see where the landscape naturally supports an off-road NCT alignment (Figure 3). At
the local scale, least-cost paths show how a candidate route might thread through these bands as
it passes through specific communities and land ownership patterns.




Figure 3. Final suitability raster layer classifying the route suitability of the North Country Trail
Corridor where green is most suitable and red is least suitable.

In the Grand Rapids area, the current NCT alignment is relatively strong compared to other
segments. Much of the trail already uses off-road corridors and passes through public land and
negotiates urban traversability constraints. The suitability model reinforces this pattern: large
swaths of favorable cells appear around the existing off-road segments, and the least-cost path
tends to follow these areas closely. The road-based segments near the city limits and within
Grand Rapids itself, however, stand out as stretches where the cost surface becomes less
favorable because of denser development, more complex ownership, and a higher density of
paved surfaces. For the city itself, we did not re-model detailed street-by-street options as, given
present constraints, road-based travel through Grand Rapids remains the most practical option.
The surrounding layers still help identify where future easements or short connecting paths might
allow the trail to reach quieter streets or improved crossings in the long term, and these are
decisions that local planners and chapter leaders can evaluate using their on-the-ground
knowledge.


                                                                                                    8
In the central Arrowhead region, where the current alignment relies heavily on roads, the
suitability model reveals a different picture. The corridor provided by NCT already directed
attention to the area north of the existing road-based route, and the model further supports this
emphasis by highlighting large areas of public and industrial forest land, along with the presence
of the Taconite State Trail. Within this band of higher suitability, the least-cost path aligns
closely with preliminary volunteer-drafted routes that have been sketched as conceptual reroutes.
This convergence between modeled and volunteer routes is encouraging, because it suggests that
the statewide datasets and suitability rules are capturing many of the same advantages and
constraints that volunteers have observed on the ground.

The Taconite State Trail itself emerged as a key feature in our analysis. The model recognizes it
as an existing corridor through public land and thus assigns favorable cost values to cells in its
vicinity. However, the trail’s mixed-use character complicates its suitability for the NCT. All
segments allow horses and bicycles, some allow all-terrain vehicles, and small sections are
paved. These attributes may not fully align with the desired characteristics of a primarily
pedestrian, natural-surface National Scenic Trail. At the same time, most of the Taconite corridor
is natural terrain or gravel, and it already has a right-of-way and management plan in place,
which can make co-location or parallel routing more feasible than securing entirely new
easements. The model does not encode these policy and experience nuances in full; instead, it
highlights where co-location might be geographically efficient, leaving detailed decisions about
how closely and for how long to share the corridor to later planning stages.

In the Ely and Snowbank Lake area, the modeling results underscore just how constrained the
corridor becomes. The composite suitability surface reveals bottlenecks where highway
crossings, hydrologic barriers, and private parcels converge. Bridge and hydrology layers
identify the crossing that can safely be used by hikers, and this location effectively functions as a
gate through which any continuous route must pass. Beyond that crossing, the candidate route
must still contend with dense private ownership and the need to weave between lakes, wetlands,
and existing infrastructure. This pattern makes it clear that purely data-driven routing has limits
in such settings and that strong partnerships will be as important as the suitability surface itself.
There is already an existing mowed utility clearing for electrical transmission that runs through
much of this area, and that corridor may offer a tangible starting point for discussions about
co-located or adjacent trail segments

Because of these constraints and the relative coarseness of the available data, the Ely segment of
the modeled route should be viewed as a first pass. It provides a helpful indication of where the
landscape and infrastructure make a continuous off-road route most feasible, but it will need to
be revisited with higher resolution terrain data, more detailed ownership information, and close
collaboration with agencies and landowners before any route is advanced toward
implementation.

V. Tools and Data Products
North Star Consulting delivered a set of geospatial tools meant to integrate smoothly into
NCTA’s existing workflow. The most direct product is a shapefile representing the modeled NCT
alignment between Grand Rapids and Snowbank Lake. This file can be loaded into ArcGIS Pro,
ArcGIS Online, or other GIS software for visualization and editing. A KML version of the same
line was also created for easy use with GPS devices and mobile apps, allowing volunteers to
download the modeled route, walk it, and record suggested edits back with project coordinators.

The project also produced a trail suitability map derived from the composite cost surface. In web
map and static form, this heat map uses a progression from softer, lighter tones for less suitable
areas to deeper, more saturated tones for highly suitable areas. When combined with parcel
layers and point of interest locations, the suitability map becomes a useful tool for designing
field visits and prioritizing which segments of the corridor warrant more detailed investigation.

A third major product is a notification-style web application built around parcels intersected by
the potential trail corridor and modeled route. This web app allows planners and volunteers to
see which landowners would potentially be affected by a given alignment and to begin
organizing outreach and negotiation strategies. As NCTA and partners refine the route, they can
update this impacted-parcels dataset to reflect new trail alignments. Additionally, the app is
designed to draw on data provided by the state so that parcel-related information stays current as
those underlying data are updated on the state’s schedule.

All of these products are hosted or compatible with ArcGIS Online, so they can be shared among
NCTA staff, chapter leaders, agency partners, and volunteers.


VI. Discussion and Recommendations
This project demonstrates how a relatively simple suitability model, when combined with
thoughtfully prepared datasets, can provide valuable guidance for a complex trail-planning
problem However, the results also highlight the importance of ground-truthing,
partnership-building, and iterative modeling.

The most immediate recommendation is to continue and formalize the iterative workflow that
volunteers have already been using. With the modeled route now available in KML format,
NCTA can encourage field teams to download a segment, hike it, record their observations and
preferred micro-reroutes, and then feed those edits back into the central geodatabase. Over time,
these corrections and refinements can be used to adjust suitability scores, revisit the weight
placed on certain features, and create a tighter fit between the model and the realities of the
landscape.


                                                                                                10
In constrained areas, particularly near Ely and along major highways, strategic partnerships will
be crucial. Utility corridors and transportation rights-of-way are sometimes the only places
where continuous, legally defensible alignments can be achieved. Planners in Minnesota have
access to a statewide right-of-way GIS resource maintained by MnDOT, which provides detailed
maps of transportation right-of-way and related information across the state. This resource,
available at https://www.dot.state.mn.us/maps/gisweb/row/, can help NCTA and partners identify
where existing ROW may offer opportunities for co-located trail segments or parallel paths that
remain within state or county control.

Another recommendation is to re-run the model in high-priority segments using higher resolution
terrain data. LiDAR-derived bare-earth digital elevation models, slope maps, and
flow-accumulation indices would allow the suitability surface to capture local landform patterns
and drainage features that the 30-meter model cannot. In wet or steep areas, this could
significantly change the relative attractiveness of alternative corridors. As higher-resolution
hydrology and land cover datasets become available, they can be added to the same modeling
framework using the existing pattern of classification, rasterization, and overlay.

It will also be valuable to experiment with alternative parcel-weighting scenarios. The current
model treats all private parcels as uniformly difficult, but in practice some landowners are
enthusiastic supporters of the trail, while others may be opposed or constrained by institutional
requirements. Running multiple scenarios, such as one that strongly avoids all private land, one
that assumes some private-land partnerships, and one that emphasizes minimizing road-walks
even at the cost of more complex negotiations, would give NCTA a richer picture of potential
tradeoffs. These scenarios could be shared with the NCTA board, agency partners, and funders to
inform strategic decisions about where to invest staff and volunteer energy.

Finally, the workflow developed in this project can be extended to other NCT gaps in Minnesota
and beyond. Because it relies primarily on publicly available datasets and documented
processing steps, it offers a scalable framework for route planning across the entire trail system.
As new data become available and as volunteers continue to walk and refine the route, the
modeling framework can be updated and reused, ensuring that the tools remain relevant as the
trail itself evolves.


VII. Conclusion
The work presented in this report represents a semester-long collaboration between the North
Country Trail Association and graduate students at the University of Minnesota. By building a
suitability model, generating candidate routes, and packaging results into accessible GIS
products, North Star Consulting has provided a regional-scale view of where a future off-road
NCT alignment between Grand Rapids and Snowbank Lake is most likely to succeed. The model



                                                                                                 11
confirms the promise of areas already being explored by volunteers, such as corridors near the
Taconite State Trail, while drawing attention to remaining bottlenecks in places like the Ely area.

At its core, the project is not about prescribing a single “correct” route, but about giving NCTA
and its partners better tools for thinking about the landscape. The maps and datasets developed
here can be used to focus ground-truthing where it will matter most, to organize outreach to
landowners and ROW managers, and to support future environmental review and funding
applications. As new data become available and as volunteers continue to walk and refine the
route, the modeling framework can be updated and extended to other segments along the North
Country Trail. In this way, the work of North Star Consulting forms one step in the long,
collective effort to turn a line on a map into a continuous, safe, and inspiring trail on the ground.
